# Private Narrative Plans — The Letter-Writer's Landing

## Assigned dialectic
**D3 — LOVE IS A DEBT (bun khun / katanyu / nii bun khun).** Care received is a loan; love arrives as principal and is repaid, with interest, forever.

## What this place reveals (unique to D3, unique to this place)
**The remittance letter as the physical form of the gratitude-debt.** Nowhere else in the world is *nii bun khun* visible as an object: here, love denominated is stacked in pigeonholes, folded around banknotes, read aloud to illiterate mothers by a paid intermediary. The institution unique to this place is the **professional letter-writer as the clerk of the debt economy** — the man through whom every absence is serviced monthly, who knows which sons send money and which send only words, whose rates rise when a letter must perform *katanyu* convincingly ("the ones that must sound dutiful"). He keeps no ledger, yet the pigeonhole rack *is* the ledger: fat envelopes and thin ones, current ones and grey ones, a dead man's name still receiving paper because the family upstream has not been told.

Why unique to D3: this is not the wat's merit-accounting (D2 — no cosmic balance, every letter names a *person* owed), not face (D1 — the contents are private, read aloud only to the debtor), not fate (D4 — nothing here is written in advance; everything here is *owed from the past*). The Pawnshop converts objects to cash; the Landing converts cash back into love and love back into obligation. The canal literally carries the debt in and out — repayment flowing uphill, as the dialectic requires.

Recurring cast available on-site: the letter-writer himself (a creditor's clerk who is himself a debtor — he writes free letters every month to a teacher in another province); the boat-mail carrier; the old women who cannot read and must have their children's love *performed* to them in public; the queue of the indebted.

## Episode hooks
1. **The dead man's letters.** A woman pays the letter-writer monthly to compose letters — and enclose money — in the name of a son who died two years ago upriver, so the boy's grandmother at the landing will not lose the will to eat. The party is asked to carry one letter and discovers the forgery. Do they expose it, take over the payments, or let the debt outlive the debtor?
2. **Calling it in (tuang bun khun).** An old patron arrives from the New Road strip with a bundle of twenty-year-old letters and dictates one new one: a demand that a former protege, now prospering, repay "twenty years of rice." The letter-writer refuses to write it — the first refusal anyone has heard from him — and the party must find out whose name is on the other end, and why the clerk of debts will not service this one.
3. **The unpaid page.** A girl saves satang by satang to have a letter written to the mother who sold her into service — not a complaint, a *repayment schedule*, offering to buy back her own bun khun in installments. The letter-writer writes it for free and lies about it. Then the mother's reply arrives in the fat-envelope pigeonhole, and no one dares read it aloud.

## Dialectical noun phrase
**"The letters that came every month like a bill"** — or, for the rack itself: **"a ledger of love, kept in pigeonholes."** (Applied flatly, as description: "She paid her mother in envelopes." "His duty arrived on the third of the month.")

## Spatial layout notes (scene tracking)
1. **The Folding Table** — the letter-writer's workstation on the landing's top step: inkstone, tin pen-box, blotting sand. Where letters are commissioned, dictated, and priced. Negotiations and refusals happen here.
2. **The Pigeonhole Rack** — the wooden shelf of awaiting envelopes against the stilt-house wall behind the table, sorted by family name. The physical ledger of who owes whom; snooping here is a scene in itself.
3. **The Canal Steps** — the worn stairs down to the water where the mail boat ties up on mail days; the crowd gathers here; arrivals, departures, and public readings begin on these steps.
4. **The Reading Bench** — a long bench under the stilt house where the old women sit to have letters read aloud. Private grief performed semi-publicly; where the party overhears what the letters actually say.
5. **The Back Room** — the letter-writer's cramped quarters under the house, reached by a ladder: unsent drafts, letters he never delivered, the free letters he writes to his own teacher, and the tin cashbox. The place's hidden archive — where the debt economy's exceptions are stored.
