# PRIVATE — Sangwan "Wan" (char_sangwan) — Dialectic D3: LOVE IS A DEBT

## Research anchor
Fused from two documented lives in the brief (Part 2):
- **The qiaopi remittance migrants (item 9)** — kept: the "silver letter" (money + letter in one envelope), the courier chain (here the canal boatman instead of the shuike "water guest"), and above all the *Dear You* structure: a person writing and remitting **in a dead relative's name for years** so the family will not lose hope. Changed: Guangdong/Fujian migration becomes internal Siam (Bangkok shophouse ↔ canal village); the dead husband becomes a dead sister.
- **Kulap Saipradit (item 6)** — kept: the poor boy schooled on a dressmaker mother's and a dancer sister's earnings; journalism; jail in the 1950s for signing the wrong article; the gratitude-debt spent on everything except the creditors. Changed: no exile to China, no novels — Wan is a small man, a veranda letter-writer, not a famous dissident. His one political act is long past and cost him exactly what it cost Kulap: the years in which the debt could still be repaid in person.

## Universal drive under the particular goal
**Goal:** keep the monthly silver letter going — money plus a letter in his sister's hand, addressed to their mother in Bangkok, four years and counting.
**Drive:** to have been worth the sacrifice — to be a son. He cannot repay the dead, so he pays the living in the dead's name; underneath the forgery is the simplest ache in the world: he wants his mother to have *somebody*. Never state this. Show it: he practices his sister's signature on scrap paper while clients dictate; he keeps her gold bracelet in a pawnshop cycle timed to the letters; he notices, unasked, every mother who comes to the veranda and which son's name she says first.

## The irony (one line)
The canal's broker of other sons' devotion — a man who services everyone's gratitude-debt for a fee — can only service his own by fraud: he is simultaneously the debtor who owes his sister his whole education and the creditor paying himself, in his own handwriting, at four satang a page.

## Three dialectical noun phrases
1. **"a creditor paid in his own handwriting"**
2. **"the canal's broker of other sons' devotion"**
3. **"a sister kept alive at four satang a page"**

## Subplot 1 — The silver letter (the boatman; the pawnshop appraiser)
**Situation:** On the first of the month Wan hands the boatman a sealed envelope — Thai baht folded into a letter in a woman's neat hand, addressed to Mae in a Bangkok shophouse alley, signed *Nim*. **Backstory revealed:** Nim was his younger sister, a lakhon dancer whose fees and whose gold bracelet paid his school fees and his first pen. She died of fever the year he was in prison; he never told their mother. **Personality revealed:** precision as devotion — the letter is never late, the handwriting never slips, and the appraiser has watched the same bracelet enter the pawnshop on the twenty-eighth and leave on the third for four years without ever asking why. **Effect on mission:** this month's envelope never arrives — the boatman was held up at the lock, and now Mae has written to the veranda asking why the money stopped. **The PC's decision:** Lek is asked to write the apology letter "for Nim." He holds the sister's signature in Wan's practice-sheets and knows it is false. Write the forgery and keep a mother fed and hopeful — or refuse, and make Wan face the default.

## Subplot 2 — Bua's last letter (Bua the old dance teacher)
**Situation:** Bua summons Wan to write a letter she dictates: to Mae in Bangkok, telling her that her daughter Nim has been dead four years. **Backstory revealed:** Nim was Bua's pupil, beaten into excellence with the counting-stick; it was Bua who prepared the body for cremation while Wan sat in a cell, and Bua who has kept the secret ever since — her own debt to the girl, she says, was never Wan's to pay by lying. She is dying and refuses to carry a false account out of the world. **Personality revealed:** Wan, who writes anyone's truth for money, begs — actually kneels — for this one truth to stay unwritten; cancellation of the debt, he says, erases the person. If Mae knows, then Nim is *only* dead. **Effect on mission:** Mae's reply to the interrupted remittance (Subplot 1) arrives: she is coming down the canal by boat to see her daughter. **The PC's decision:** Bua, too weak to hold the pen, asks Lek to write the truth letter in her stead. Write it — or help Wan hire a dancer to sit veiled in a dim house for one afternoon, so the debt can go on being paid.

## Subplot 3 — The officer's ledger (the young district officer; the monk)
**Situation:** The young district officer offers Wan a clean record — the prison stain struck from the district rolls — in exchange for copies of the veranda's letter traffic: who sends money where, whose sons write from which city. **Backstory revealed:** the prison term came from one signed article; Wan was never brave, only tired of writing other people's courage. Meanwhile the monk tells Lek that a jar of unclaimed bone-ash has sat at the wat four years — a dancer, no kin came — and an unmerited ghost is bad for the whole canal. Only a relative can make merit for her, and a woman still alive on paper cannot have merit made. **Personality revealed:** Wan refuses the officer in a voice so cool it sounds like agreement, then gets drunk for the only time anyone has seen — heat he hides even from himself. **Effect on mission:** the officer, refused, begins copying the letter-book himself; every remittance family on the canal is now legible to the district. **The PC's decision:** the monk needs a name for the merit ceremony. Lek can give it — ending the silver letters, freeing the ashes, protecting no one — or say nothing and let Wan decide alone whether his sister is finally allowed to die.

## Voice notes
Speaks in stationery. Salutations instead of greetings — he will begin a spoken sentence "To the respected—" and catch himself. Quotes his clients' letters back at them months later, word-perfect. Prices everything in pages and postage; when moved, he becomes more formal, not less (the hotter the heart, the cooler the salutation). Never says "my sister"; says "the lady Nim," "the elder of the two of us," or "the hand I copy." Drinks only after the last envelope is sealed, and while drinking recites the correct forms of address for letters to officials, monks, and mothers — a liturgy. His one obscenity is the debt-collector's verb: *tuang* — "they called it in" — used for jail, for fever, for Bua's counting-stick, for everything that ever took anything from him.
