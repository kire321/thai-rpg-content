# PRIVATE — The Pawnshop Appraiser (char_pawnshop_appraiser) — Dialectic D3: LOVE IS A DEBT

## Logical implication of Wan's contradiction
Wan is a creditor paid in his own handwriting: he services his own gratitude-debt by fraud, and knows that canceling the debt would erase the relationship (if Mae learns Nim is dead, the payments stop, and then Nim is *only* dead). The Appraiser is the generic, smaller instance of the same shape, one implication downstream:

**If/then:** *If a canceled debt erases the relationship, then a creditor who never lets the debt close keeps the debtor forever.* She has run a pawnshop forty years and quietly understands what her trade actually sells: not loans, but scheduled returns. The pledge is an excuse for the debtor to come back. So she extends tickets she should call, lowers interest for the regulars she likes, and — her one fixed rule — grows cold and exact the moment anyone tries to settle *in full and forever*. Full payment is a door closing. She would rather hold a bracelet for forty years than be handed the last satang and lose the face that came with it.

She is NOT Wan: she writes nothing, forges nothing, services no one else's debt. She is the counter Wan's bracelet passes through — the pure creditor side of the contradiction, standing still while the fraud walks in and out of her shop on schedule.

## Function the party repeatedly needs
Pawn and redemption of valuables (quick cash, storing a dangerous object, retrieving a seized or stolen item that passed through her counter); informal credit against collateral; and her ticket-ledger, which is the canal's quiet archive of who was desperate, when, and for how much — information she gives only to faces she has decided to keep.

## Episode hooks
1. **The bracelet on the twenty-eighth.** A regular client — a polite letter-writer from the veranda — pawns the same woman's gold bracelet every month on the twenty-eighth and redeems it on the third, four years running, the ticket always made out in a lady's name. The Appraiser never asks; she only noticed long ago that the bracelet is older than the hand that brings it, and that the redemption money smells of ink. She hires the party to quietly make sure nothing has happened to "the lady" — not out of suspicion of Wan, but because if the lady were gone, the bracelet would one day simply not be redeemed, and she does not want to learn that from an empty counter. (Intersects Wan's silver-letter secret without her knowing it: the party may hold the answer she is paying them not to find.)
2. **The last satang.** An old farmer, dying, sends his son to redeem the family amulet and close his account *in full* — forty years of tickets, every debt settled. The Appraiser refuses to compute the final figure: the scales are off, the gold price is wrong, come back next week. The party is asked by the son to make her take the money. If they press her, they find the truth: she is not cheating him. Settling in full means the farmer's family never has a reason to climb her steps again, and she has buried more creditors than she has grandchildren.
3. **The ticket-ledger.** Someone official (a district clerk, a debt-collector, a police sergeant — whoever the episode needs) wants her forty years of pawn tickets: they map every household's hunger better than any census. She will not sell the ledger itself, but she will trade single pages for favors — and the party needs one page (a pawned weapon, a stolen ring, proof a family could not have paid a fine). Whatever page they buy teaches her exactly what the party is hunting.

## Three dialectical noun phrases
1. **"a creditor who refuses the last satang"**
2. **"forty years of other people's gold, none of it hers to keep and all of it hers to keep waiting"**
3. **"interest charged on a face she likes"**

## Voice notes
Speaks in weights and dates. Never says a customer is poor — says "the ticket is old," "the gold has slept here long." Prices affection as lower interest and never explains the discount. Her phrase for death, default, and departure is the same: "the ticket expired." When she likes someone she offers tea and a longer redemption window in the same breath, as if they were one courtesy. She addresses everyone by their pledge, not their name — "mother of the pearl ring," "the lieutenant's chain" — because in her shop a person *is* what they owe, and she finds this warmer than names, which can be changed.
