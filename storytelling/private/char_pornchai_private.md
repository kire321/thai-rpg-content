# char_pornchai — Narrative Blueprint

**Research Anchor & Adaptation**  
Chit Phumisak (1930–1965): radical philologist, imprisoned for dissent, later posthumously canonized by the state he criticized. His life embodies the dialectic’s core tension—using the coldest intellectual tools (language study, archival rigor) in service of a fiercely hot revolutionary heart. From this, we keep: the scholar-prisoner duality, the weaponization of language, the state’s ironic embrace of suppressed thought. Changed: Pom is not a rebel in action but in silence; his rebellion is deferred, internalized. He never joins an insurgency but *could have*. He writes not manifestos but letters—correcting grammar, refining tone, excising emotional heat. His prison is not cells but composure.

---

**Drive & Irony**  
*Universal Drive:* To be heard without shouting.  
*Concrete Goal:* To compile a perfect, neutral dictionary of village speech—words as they are, not as they should be.  
*Irony:* The man who speaks with the coolest restraint carries the hottest grief—his words are preserved, but his brother’s voice was burned with his body in a prison yard.

---

**Practice / Inventory / Attention**  
- *Practice:* Every evening, Pom burns one page of his notebook in the temple brazier. He does not watch it burn.  
- *Inventory:* His steel pen (ink always black), a folded government release stating his brother “died in custody,” and 173 recorded variants of the word *jai* (“heart,” “mind,” “spirit”) from local speech.  
- *Attention:* Notices when someone corrects their own word choice mid-sentence. Counts syllables in arguments. Records tone shifts in disputes. Never looks directly at fire.

---

**Three Dialectical Noun Phrases**  
1. *a letter gone cold*  
2. *a word gone subzero*  
3. *a grief run to fever*

## Subplot 1: *The Letter That Would Not Be Sent*

**Situation**  
A widow brings a letter to Pom, meant for her son in Bangkok—pleading him to return for her funeral. She’s dying, but insists the letter be sent only after she’s gone. Pom reads it, corrects the spelling, and holds it. Days pass. The district officer asks why it’s delayed. Pom says, “It’s not ready.”  

**Backstory Revealed**  
Pom’s brother, a student radical, wrote a final letter from prison. Pom never received it. The state released a sanitized version years later—“regrettable incident,” “unfortunate misunderstanding”—but the real letter, he believes, was burned. His brother’s last words were never sent.  

**Personality Revealed**  
Pom does not believe in posthumous speech. To send a letter after death is to let silence speak for you—and silence, he knows, can be rewritten. He fears the son will receive not his mother’s voice, but a version of it, shaped by Pom’s own hand.  

**Effect on Mission**  
The widow’s funeral approaches. The letter remains unsent. The boatman refuses to carry it until it’s sealed. The monk suggests cremating it with her. The PC must decide: send it, burn it, or rewrite it.  

**PC’s Decision**  
Lek must choose whether to override Pom and send the letter as-is, burn it with Pom in the brazier, or draft a new version that “protects” the son from grief. Each choice fractures trust with someone. Pom says nothing. He hands over the pen.

## Subplot 2: *The Grammar of Grief*

**Situation**  
The dance teacher Bua collapses during a lesson. At the veranda, she whispers a phrase in an old dialect—“*jai ron khaeng din*”—heart hot as baked earth. Pom writes it down. The district officer mocks it as “superstitious noise.” Pom corrects him: “It’s not metaphor. It’s measurement.”  

**Backstory Revealed**  
Pom’s brother used the same phrase in a smuggled poem. It was the last line the censors didn’t cut. Pom knows the heat of the heart is not poetic—it’s diagnostic. His brother died of fever in solitary, untreated. The state called it “natural causes.”  

**Personality Revealed**  
Pom believes language can kill. A misworded petition got his brother arrested. A softened report got him forgotten. He now polices every syllable, not for beauty, but for survival. He won’t speak the phrase aloud. He writes it only in cipher.  

**Effect on Mission**  
Bua’s students demand a public performance to “cool the village’s heart.” The district officer bans it, calling it “incitement.” Pom is asked to draft a neutral appeal. But any appeal, he knows, will be read for heat.  

**PC’s Decision**  
Lek must submit a petition. Pom offers three versions: one cold (bureaucratic), one warm (emotive), one silent (blank). Lek chooses. Pom burns the unused drafts. The performance is allowed—but only if no one speaks.

## Subplot 3: *The Dictionary of What Was Said*

**Situation**  
Pom is found compiling a ledger of every word spoken on the veranda over six months. Lek discovers it: not summaries, but exact transcriptions—tone, pause, correction. He confronts Pom: “This isn’t record-keeping. It’s surveillance.”  

**Backstory Revealed**  
In prison, Pom’s brother was forced to sign a confession written by guards. He didn’t speak the words. Pom now believes only *exact* speech can resist theft. But he also knows exactness breeds danger. So he records everything—then burns it. The dictionary is never complete. It cannot be.  

**Personality Revealed**  
Pom does not trust memory. He trusts only what is written, but fears what writing becomes. His entire practice is a loop: preserve, then destroy. He is not archiving history—he is performing penance for surviving it.  

**Effect on Mission**  
A land dispute arises. Both sides claim the other lied on the veranda. Lek needs a record. Pom says he has one. But to reveal it would break his vow. To withhold it is to let injustice stand.  

**PC’s Decision**  
Lek must decide whether to force Pom to hand over the ledger. Pom offers a compromise: he will read the entry aloud—once—then burn the page. Lek must listen, remember, and act. The truth is spoken. The page turns to ash. The dispute is settled. Pom does not write it down.

## Voice Notes

>  
> “They say I’m calm. But calm is not cold. Calm is the skin over the boil.”  
>  
> “My brother’s last word was ‘remember.’ But what? The word, or the voice?”  
>  
> “When they burned his body, they said it was to stop disease. But I think they were afraid the voice would walk home.”  
>  
> “I don’t fear anger. I fear the silence after. That’s when the state writes your story for you.”  
>  
> “Every time I correct a spelling, I feel him watching. Not judging. Just… measuring the heat.”

## When NOT Foregrounded

Pom sits at the edge of the veranda, spine straight, notebook open. He does not look up when others argue, but his pen moves. When Wan writes a letter, Pom waits until he’s done, then quietly places a single stroke through one word—never explains. During disputes, he pours tea for everyone, but his own cup remains empty. If someone shouts, he stands, walks to the brazier, and feeds it a scrap of paper. No one knows what’s written on it.
