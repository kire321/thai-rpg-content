## Act 1 — The Red Ink

The Funeral Timekeeper stands on the school veranda with a red-ink correction in hand, and I know right away it’s about the widow’s husband. I hear the soft tap of his sandal against the wood, and I look up to see a small man in grey cloth, holding a ledger open to a line crossed out in red, then rewritten in black. He steps forward without greeting. The Funeral Timekeeper says the death was at 4:07 AM, not 4:13, and the land audit begins tomorrow. I ask why the record changed now, six days after the coffin left. He taps the red ink: the tide log shows the boat arrived at 4:08, so the body must have died earlier. That’s no problem, he says — the water doesn’t lie. [[tag_288]] I feel the weight of his words, but the doubt stays. “Correct the time or the land goes to the district,” he says, voice flat. Kamnan Noi adjusts his compass silently, recording the exact minute the Timekeeper arrived. I look at the tin box where the school’s records sleep. I have a class to teach soon, but the box calls louder. [[tag_330]] The Funeral Timekeeper leans in: “She needs it true. But only if it’s written.” I say I’ll need proof — a tide stamp, a boat log. He says the boatman burned his log. “He said the water lied.” I hear that, and I’m happy to hear it — because if the water lied, then maybe the ink can too. [[tag_089]]

## Act 2 — The Salt and the Fire

Present: Lek, Kamnan Noi, The Funeral Timekeeper. The Boatman enters barefoot, salt-crusted shirt clinging to his shoulders, carrying a splintered oar wrapped in cloth. He says he burned the log because the widow paid him double to lie. I ask why he’s telling me now. He says the fire scared him — the house fell at 3:58, same as the tide. Kamnan Noi writes the fire’s time in his small ledger. The Salt Clerk arrives, barefoot, holding a pink salt chit. She is a woman with cracked heels, pressing a chit into the Boatman’s palm. She says the widow traded her last rice chits for salt the night before the fire. I ask if she saw the clock. She says she doesn’t read time — only salt — but the lantern was lit when she left. The Pawnshop Appraiser walks up, holding a deed. He is a man in a stiff collar, waving a deed with a pawn string still tied. He says the land was already mortgaged — the widow knew she’d lose. I ask why she burned the house. The Pawnshop Appraiser says, "Maybe she wanted the truth to burn too." That’s really easy to guess, I think — but I say nothing. [[tag_083]]  

"Whose time do you write?"  
[easy] File the widow’s version PASS: "I write 4:07 and the Appraiser pockets the deed." FAIL: "I write 4:07 and the Boatman spits on the floor."  
[medium] Record both times PASS: "I write both times and the Salt Clerk nods." FAIL: "I write both times and the Appraiser tears the page."  
[hard] Hand the ledger to Kamnan Noi PASS: "I hand him the ledger and he writes nothing." FAIL: "I hand him the ledger and he walks away."  

## Act 3 — The Stopped Clock

Present: Lek, Kamnan Noi, The Funeral Timekeeper, The Boatman, The Pawnshop Appraiser. The Monk arrives, whispering to the Appraiser. He is a man in saffron, fingers pressed together, not looking at the fire site. The Monk says the widow’s son will inherit if the time is 4:07. I ask why he cares. He says the boy is his nephew — born under Rahu. The Boatman says the naming was already carved — on the prow, in the salt. The Pawnshop Appraiser says the naming means nothing if the land is gone. I look at Kamnan Noi — he is writing in his sealed ledger. I don’t feel the same as before — something in the air has shifted. [[tag_250]] The Fire Stoker arrives, covered in ash. He is a man with soot on his arms, holding a bent clock hand. He says the clock stopped at 3:58 — same as Kamnan’s record. I ask if that proves the fire started then. The Fire Stoker says, "Time burns. But paper lies."  

"Do you burn the record or save it?"

## Act 4 — The Rising Water

Present: Lek, Kamnan Noi. The rain starts — soft on the veranda roof. I open my notebook — the word “sequence” is underlined. Kamnan Noi places his brass compass on the table, pointing west. He says, "I have something to tell you." I wait. I’m not finished yet, I say — the words sharp between us. [[tag_059]] He says nothing else — only slides the key to the sealed ledger across the wood. He says, "Time is." He walks to the riverbank. I watch him stand at the edge — water rising. …standing right in front of the flood. [[tag_198]] I pocket the key. The rain keeps falling.
